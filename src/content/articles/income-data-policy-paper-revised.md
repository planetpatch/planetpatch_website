*A Policy Paper on Distributional Evidence in Arizona Ratemaking and the Case Against Formula Rate Adoption Under Current Economic Conditions*

---

## Executive Summary

This paper advances two related recommendations for the Arizona Corporation Commission.

First, the Commission should formally incorporate aggregate income and energy-burden data into its application of the constitutional "just and reasonable" standard. Commission Staff has argued in the pending Arizona Public Service rate proceeding that affordability is not the governing legal standard and that the Commission cannot examine individual household budgets. The first proposition is correct as a matter of law. The second is correct but immaterial, because no party has proposed individualized means-testing. The relevant question is whether aggregate distributional evidence is admissible and probative in determining whether a rate is just. This paper concludes that it is, that the governing case law contemplates it, that the record in this very proceeding demonstrates its value, and that its systematic exclusion produces a ratemaking process that is regressive.

Second, the Commission should decline to adopt the proposed Formula Rate Adjustment Mechanism at this time. Formula rates function as an automatic nominal escalator. Their adoption is least defensible when consumer prices are experiencing inflationary pressure. Current pressures include, but are not limited to, the war in Iran, high U.S. Treasury Bond Yields, and tariffs. A mechanism designed to index utility revenue to cost should not be adopted in an environment where household income is not similarly indexed. The record in this case quantifies that gap directly: projected wage and salary growth for low- and middle-income households falls well below the roughly 14 percent average increase the Company seeks.

---

## I. Scope and Framing

The Commission is presently considering an APS application seeking approximately $662 million in additional base revenue, a residential impact in the range of 14 to 15 percent, and structural approval of a formula rate mechanism that would adjust most non-fuel revenue annually. Thirty-five parties intervened. The evidentiary hearing consumed thirty-one days.

The record produced by that proceeding is extensive on cost and comparatively thin on consequence. Rate base, accumulated depreciation, deferred taxes, cost of capital, and class cost allocation were litigated in detail by every party. The question of what the resulting rates do to the households paying them was developed by a single intervenor, and in Staff's brief was addressed primarily through a legal argument that the question is improper.

That asymmetry is the problem this paper addresses. The issue is not that distributional evidence is impossible to produce. The Residential Utility Consumer Office produced it in this case, and the analysis is discussed in Part II below. The issue is that its production depends on whether a particular intervenor happens to fund a particular expert in a particular proceeding, rather than on a standing requirement applicable to every general rate case.

This paper does not contend that the Commission should set rates below cost, disregard the utility's financial integrity, or substitute affordability for the constitutional standard. It contends that the constitutional standard is not satisfied by cost calculation alone, and that the Commission should not have to rely on the litigation choices of individual intervenors to obtain the evidence that standard requires.

## II. The Legal Framework Permits What Staff's Brief Forecloses

### A. The governing standard

Article 15, Section 3 of the Arizona Constitution directs the Commission to prescribe just and reasonable rates and charges. Arizona additionally requires consideration of the fair value of utility property.

Staff's Opening Brief characterizes this framework as requiring fairness to both consumers and the utility, not merely the lowest possible customer bill. That characterization is accurate and is the starting point for the analysis that follows.

### B. Staff's brief focuses on fair value and utility property

Staff's brief and the utility's testimony both rely extensively on the investor side of that balance, and the record contains detailed evidence on credit ratings, capital attraction, proxy group comparability, and market-to-book ratios.

In Staff's presentation, the consumer side of the balance is asserted rather than evidenced. A balancing test in which one side is quantified to the basis point and the other is addressed by argument is not a balancing test.

The consumer side was not left entirely undeveloped in this proceeding, however, and what was developed illustrates precisely why the requirement proposed in Part III is warranted. The Residential Utility Consumer Office retained Stanley Reynolds, Professor Emeritus of Economics at the University of Arizona, to study affordability and energy burden in APS's service territory using an anonymized review of the Company's own billing data.

The study produced findings of exactly the kind this paper argues the Commission should have before it in every case:

- Costs for housing, food, and health care have risen substantially over the past five to six years, and incomes for many low- and middle-income families have not kept pace. Home energy costs consume a significant share of income for these households.

- **Projected wage and salary growth for low- and middle-income households in 2025 and 2026 falls well below the approximately 14 percent average rate increase the Company has requested.** This single comparison states the affordability problem more precisely than any general appeal to economic conditions. A rate increase substantially outpacing income growth does not merely raise bills; it raises energy burden, which is the ratio of the two.

- The analysis focused on households at or below median income, where affordability pressure concentrates. For these households, the proposed increase would worsen affordability specifically during July and August.

- For households with incomes between 60 and 90 percent of median income, the increase would push average summer energy burden close to the five percent threshold used in the analysis.

- The increase would be unaffordable for low-income customers and for many middle-income customers. Customers eligible for the Customer Energy Support Program but not enrolled, and customers whose incomes place them just above eligibility, would be affected most acutely.

Three observations follow.

First, the analysis was feasible. It relied on the utility's own billing data combined with publicly available income statistics. Objections that distributional analysis is impracticable are not supported by what actually occurred in this case.

Second, its findings are material. Peak-season burden approaching a recognized affordability threshold for a substantial segment of residential customers is directly relevant to whether the resulting rate is just.

Third, and most importantly for the recommendation that follows, the Commission received this analysis only because one intervenor chose to fund it. Had RUCO allocated its resources differently, the record would contain nothing on the subject and Staff's legal argument that the question is improper would have gone unanswered by evidence. A constitutional standard should not depend on that contingency.

### C. Aggregate distributional evidence is not individualized means-testing

Staff's brief states that the Commission cannot examine the household budgets of a million and a half ratepayers to determine who can pay easily and who cannot. This is true and no party proposed otherwise.

However, aggregate distributional analysis, in which a regulator examines the statistical relationship between a proposed rate and the income distribution of the affected class, is standard analytical practice across regulatory domains. It is also what was done in this case: the Reynolds study examined anonymized billing data against income distributions, and identified no individual customer.

The Commission already performs analogous aggregate analysis routinely: class cost-of-service studies allocate costs across customer groups using statistical load characteristics without examining any individual customer's usage. There is no principled basis on which load data is aggregable and income data is not.

### D. "Just" is not a synonym for "cost-based"

If "just and reasonable" meant only that rates recover prudently incurred costs plus a market return, the constitutional text would be redundant of the accounting exercise. The word "just" performs independent work. It imports a fairness constraint that cannot be evaluated without reference to the party on whom the rate falls.

This does not convert the Commission into a social welfare agency. It means that a rate whose distributional consequences are unknown cannot be affirmatively found just, because the finding requires information the record does not contain.

## III. Proposed Standard: A Required Distributional Impact Assessment

The Commission should require, as a standard filing requirement in every general rate case and in connection with any proposed automatic adjustment mechanism, a Distributional Impact Assessment containing the following elements. The analysis performed in this case establishes that each element is achievable with data the utility already maintains.

**1. Energy burden by income band.** Average annual electricity expenditure as a percentage of household income, disaggregated by income decile or by bands relative to Area Median Income, presented both at current rates and at proposed rates.

The filing should also report projected wage and income growth for the affected bands over the period the rates will be in effect, so that the change in burden — not merely the change in bill — is visible on the face of the analysis. In this proceeding, that comparison showed projected income growth for low- and middle-income households running well below the approximately 14 percent average increase sought.

The energy-affordability literature commonly treats burdens exceeding approximately six percent of household income as indicative of affordability stress, and the analysis submitted in this case applied a five percent threshold. The Commission need not adopt either figure as a decision rule to benefit from seeing where the distribution falls relative to them.

**2. Peak-month burden.** The same analysis computed for the highest-consumption billing month rather than the annual average. Arizona consumption is severely seasonal, and a summer bill may approach double a winter bill for the same household. Annual averages systematically understate the acute burden, which is the burden that produces non-payment and disconnection. The evidence in this case bears this out: the increase was found to worsen affordability specifically in July and August, and to push summer burden toward the applicable threshold for households between 60 and 90 percent of median income.

**3. Arrearage and disconnection series.** Total residential arrearages, number of accounts more than thirty and sixty days past due, disconnection notices issued, and disconnections executed, reported monthly for the preceding thirty-six months and compared against the effective dates of prior rate increases.

**4. Assistance program participation against eligibility.** The number of customers enrolled in each bill assistance program measured against the estimated eligible population, with any material participation gap explained. A program serving a small fraction of its eligible population is not evidence that need is small. The record in this case identifies eligible-but-unenrolled customers as among those most severely affected, which makes the size of that gap a material fact rather than an administrative detail.

**5. Geographic disaggregation.** Results reported at the county level at minimum. Statewide averages conceal substantial variation; Yuma County's unemployment rate exceeded 16 percent in mid-2026 while Greenlee County's was under 4 percent, and a uniform statewide rate increase does not land uniformly.

**6. Post-implementation reporting.** For any approved increase, the utility should report items 1 through 3 at twelve and twenty-four months following the effective date, permitting the Commission to observe whether its predictive assumptions held.

### Use of the assessment

The Commission should not treat the assessment as a cap or as a competing standard. It should treat it as a required evidentiary predicate to the just-and-reasonable finding, informing:

- Rate design, including the allocation between fixed basic service charges and volumetric rates, since fixed charges are inherently regressive relative to income;
- The pace of movement toward full cost-of-service alignment for particular classes;
- The scale and structure of assistance programs; and
- Whether a proposed increase should be phased rather than implemented at once.

Each of those determinations already lies within the Commission's discretion. The assessment supplies the factual basis for exercising it.

## IV. Formula Rates Are Poorly Suited to Present Economic Conditions

### A. What the mechanism does

The proposed Formula Rate Adjustment Mechanism would permit APS to update substantially all non-fuel revenue requirements annually through a formula, with rates adjusting unless a challenge succeeds within a compressed procedural window. Staff supports the mechanism with modifications, including a fifty basis point deadband and Commission approval of resulting changes.

Functionally, the mechanism indexes utility revenue to the utility's own cost structure on an annual cycle. Its effect is to transfer inflation and cost-escalation risk from shareholders to ratepayers, and to convert what has historically been an episodic, contested proceeding into a recurring administrative one.

### B. The asymmetry this creates

Formula rates are frequently defended as symmetric: rates may fall as well as rise. In practice the symmetry is weak, and current conditions weaken it further.

The mechanism escalates with the utility's costs, which are rising with general inflation and with an unprecedented capital program driven by large load interconnection. Household income, the denominator of energy burden, is not escalating at a comparable rate. That is not a matter of inference in this proceeding. Projected wage and salary growth for low- and middle-income households was shown to fall well below the roughly 14 percent increase now sought, and the mechanism would compound increases of this character annually thereafter.

The mechanism therefore does not merely pass inflation through. It widens the gap between utility revenue growth and household capacity to absorb it, every year, without a contested proceeding in which that gap must be justified. A single increase outpacing income growth raises energy burden once. An annual mechanism raises it repeatedly, and the effect accumulates.

### C. Regulatory lag is a counter-cyclical cost control

Regulatory lag operates automatically and without litigation. Between rate cases, incremental spending reduces shareholder returns until rates reset, creating a continuous incentive toward cost discipline. Compressing that interval to twelve months substantially attenuates the incentive.

That attenuation is most costly precisely when capital programs are largest and least well-tested by experience. APS currently faces an interconnection queue approaching seventeen gigawatts, driven overwhelmingly by data center demand, against residential load growth of approximately 0.4 percent annually. The investment program this generates is forecast-dependent and carries material stranded-cost risk. Reducing the automatic discipline applicable to that program, while simultaneously guaranteeing annual recovery, inverts the appropriate allocation of risk.

## V. Recommendations

**1.** Adopt a Distributional Impact Assessment as a standard filing requirement in general rate cases, containing the six elements specified in Part III, with post-implementation reporting at twelve and twenty-four months. The assessment should be a utility filing obligation rather than a matter left to intervenor initiative.

**2.** Issue a policy statement confirming that aggregate income and energy-burden evidence is admissible and probative under the just-and-reasonable standard, and that its consideration does not constitute individualized means-testing or displacement of the constitutional standard.

**3.** Decline to adopt the Formula Rate Adjustment Mechanism in the pending proceeding. Revisit the question in a separate generic docket in which the mechanism's quantified customer impact can be developed on a full record.

**4.** If the mechanism is nonetheless adopted, condition it on: an affirmative Commission decision before any rate change takes effect; pre-filed sworn testimony supporting each update; and a procedural schedule affording challengers a meaningful review period.

**5.** Require seasonally disaggregated bill impact disclosure to customers before any approved increase takes effect, stating the expected peak-month impact alongside the annual average.

**6.** Direct the utility to report annually on assistance program participation against estimated eligibility, and maintain crisis assistance funding at no less than current levels pending that reporting.

---

## VI. Conclusion

The Commission's constitutional mandate requires rates that are just, not merely rates that are arithmetically derived from cost. A finding of justness that rests on no evidence about the households bearing the rate is a finding made without a necessary predicate.

This proceeding shows both that such evidence can be produced and what it reveals when it is. It also shows that its production currently depends on the resources and priorities of a single intervenor. The remedy proposed here is evidentiary rather than substantive. It does not tell the Commission what to decide. It requires that the Commission know, before deciding, what its decision will do — in every case, and not only in those where someone else pays to find out.

On formula rates, the recommendation is narrower still: not that automatic adjustment mechanisms are categorically improper, but that adopting one while household income growth trails the requested increase places the resulting risk on the party least able to bear it, at the moment they are least able to bear it.
